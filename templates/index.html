Interactive Threat Explainer: "What Just Happened?"

Understand how an attacker exploits each vulnerability in plain English, with observable facts vs. risk analysis.
Scenario: You approve a dApp with an unlimited allowance (2^256 - 1)

The Mechanism: The transaction executes approve(spender, 115792089237316195423570985008687907853269984665640564039457584007913129639935). This instructs the token contract's internal mapping allowed[yourAddress][spender] to store maximum uint256.

🚨 Attack Timeline: What Happens If The Spender Is Malicious Or Compromised?
Step 1 (Legitimate Intent): You sign the approval in your wallet expecting to trade 100 USDC.
Step 2 (Dormant Permission): The allowance stays permanently saved on the blockchain with no expiration date.
Step 3 (The Exploit): Months later, the spender router is compromised by an attacker, or was an unverified phishing contract.
Step 4 (Silent Asset Sweep): The attacker invokes transferFrom(yourAddress, attackerAddress, balance). Your entire USDC wallet balance is drained without any new wallet prompt.
Observable Fact vs. Risk
Fact: Spender allowance is 2^256 - 1. An unlimited approval doesn't prove bad intent, but eliminates all safety margins if the spender is ever breached.
Recommended Safe Action
Always customize the allowance in your wallet to the exact amount needed (e.g. 100 USDC), or revoke permissions after trading.
Scenario: Smart contract deploys with an unprotected mint() or withdraw() function

The Mechanism: The function mint(uint256 amount) public increases totalSupply += amount or modifies balances, but has NO modifier (like onlyOwner) or require(msg.sender == owner) check.

🚨 Attack Timeline: What Happens When An Attacker Calls It?
Step 1 (Discovery): Attacker scans newly deployed contracts on block explorers and finds a public mint() function with no restrictions.
Step 2 (Free Token Creation): Attacker sends a direct transaction: mint(10,000,000) directly to their address.
Step 3 (EVM Execution): The EVM executes the code unconditionally because the developer forgot the authorization gate.
Step 4 (Market Crash): Attacker dumps the newly minted tokens onto Uniswap pools, draining all paired ETH and rendering honest holders' tokens worthless.
Observable Fact vs. Risk
Fact: The function is public and modifies state variables without caller verification. In Solidity, public functions are anyone-callable by default.
Recommended Safe Action
Inherit OpenZeppelin's Ownable or AccessControl: declare function mint(...) public onlyOwner or check role permissions.
Scenario: Contract performs external call (msg.sender.call) before updating balance records

The Mechanism: The contract runs (bool s, ) = msg.sender.call{value: amount}("") BEFORE executing balances[msg.sender] = 0;. This transfers EVM execution control flow to the recipient before updating storage.

🚨 Attack Timeline: What Happens During Recursive Callback?
Step 1 (Setup): Attacker contract deposits 1 ETH into the vault. Vault records balances[attacker] = 1 ETH.
Step 2 (Withdrawal Request): Attacker calls withdraw(). Vault checks balances[attacker] == 1 ETH (passes) and executes msg.sender.call{value: 1 ETH}.
Step 3 (The Interception): Attacker's contract has a receive() external payable fallback function that immediately calls vault.withdraw() AGAIN before the original call finishes!
Step 4 (Outdated State Exploit): On re-entry, the vault checks balances[attacker]. Because the line balances[msg.sender] = 0 hasn't executed yet, the vault still reads 1 ETH and sends another 1 ETH! Repeated until the entire contract is drained.
Observable Fact vs. Risk
Fact: The external value transfer precedes the storage write in the control flow graph. Reentrancy occurs when code trusts un-updated state across execution boundaries.
Recommended Safe Action
Apply Checks-Effects-Interactions (zero balance BEFORE calling), or use OpenZeppelin's ReentrancyGuard with nonReentrant modifier.
